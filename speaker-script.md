# Le Dossier IA: speaker script (about 5 minutes)

**How to drive it.** There's no control bar: use the **right and left arrows** (or space). Each slide has three parts, so it's **two clicks per slide**, and each part plays by itself. The thin strip along the top of the bottom band is your **timeline**: blue segments are done, red is where you are, grey is still to come, with a gap between sections (slides 1–3, slide 4, the video and Merci, the annexes). Click a segment to jump there. Keys: **F** full screen (press again to leave), **N** notes, **Q** Ask Claude, **1–3** slides, **4** slide 4, **E** the video.

Words in **bold** get a little more stress. Your name and the exercise question sit in the band at the bottom of every slide, so the room never loses the thread.

## Slide 1, Le cadrage (the brief)

**Part 1: the cover. Blue, the white poster, red: the French flag.**

"Hi everyone. Let me start with a quick reminder of **the exercise**, in case that part of Session 9 slipped by.

The professor gave us a scenario. You work for **the government**. You used **generative AI**, the same kind of tool as ChatGPT or an image generator, to make an ad: both the words and the picture. The ad teaches **money basics**, what the lecture calls financial literacy, to people on **lower incomes**.

Then an **algorithm** decides who sees the ad. It's an automatic ad buyer: it tries to reach as many of those people as possible for as little money as possible.

Our job has two steps. First, **find what could go wrong**. Second, **map each risk**: who gets hurt, and how badly. And we get three slides at most.

Now, the brief just says 'the government'. I live in **France**, so I made it the **French** government. That's realistic: France really does run financial education. The Banque de France leads a national strategy and runs a public website for it. So this poster, '1 euro a day is 365 euros a year', is the kind of ad they could make. To be clear, **I made this poster for the deck**. It's not a real campaign."

**Part 2 (click): the two machines.**

"Here's the whole set-up in one picture. The ad goes through **two machines**.

Machine one is **the writer**: the AI that makes the poster. Machine two is **the buyer**: the algorithm that buys space on people's screens, like the phones you see in this métro carriage. And at the end is the person the ad is for, someone like the people **queueing at the CAF**, the French office that pays family and housing benefits.

Now look at the red dotted line in the middle. **Nobody checks the ad** between the two machines. Keep that in mind; it comes back at the end.

And the campaign is worth doing: **9.8 million** people in France live below the poverty line, only **56 %** of adults have basic financial knowledge, and **76 experiments** show that teaching money skills actually works."

**Part 3 (click): the eight risks.**

"So, what could go wrong? I found **eight risks**, and each one starts in one of the two machines.

The writer brings five. For example, it could draw a **stereotyped picture** of poor people, or print a **wrong figure** that makes the ad look like a scam.

The buyer brings three, and these are the **serious ones**, as you'll see on the next slide.

Each risk carries the tags from the lecture. **Inherited** means the problem comes with the AI tool we bought; **added** means our own choices created it. **Deliberate** means someone chose it; **accidental** means nobody meant it. Remember that last pair, because it decides the fix."

## Slide 2, Le mécanisme (how the buyer goes wrong)

**Part 1: the price tags on the métro tiles. The circles fill by themselves.**

"Let me explain the buyer in plain words, because this is the heart of it.

Every time the ad appears on someone's screen, the ministry **pays a small fee**. That's the cost. And the fee depends on the person.

Someone who's on their phone **all day** is easy to reach, so they're **cheap**: about **8 cents**. Someone who's **rarely online** (shared phone, weak signal) is hard to find, so they cost **four times more**: 32 cents.

The buyer gets **240,000 euros** and one instruction: reach as many people as possible. So watch the order it buys in."

*(Pause while the circles fill.)*

"It buys the **cheapest people first**: all the blue ones. Then the green ones. And the money runs out **before a single orange circle**. *Jamais atteints*: never reached."

**Part 2 (click): the map and the green report.**

"Here's where the ad landed, and the weekly report the ministry would get. **2.25 million** people reached, **75 percent** of the target, **11 cents** each. Every number is **green**. The campaign looks like a success."

**Part 3 (click): the red screen.**

"Now split that same report **by group**. The people who are rarely online, the very people this campaign is for, got **zero percent**. Like the people of Mafate on La Réunion, an area with no roads at all.

And this has happened for real. A neutral ad for **science careers** was shown to fewer women, because **younger women cost more to reach**. Facebook's ad system skewed **job and housing ads** by gender and race.

The lecture has a name for this: **optimising the wrong metric**. The algorithm did its job perfectly. **The job was wrong.**

And look at the stamp on the photo. France's motto is *liberté, égalité, fraternité*. **Égalité**, equality. This buyer gave the people who need the ad most **zero**."

## Slide 3, La matrice (the risk map and the fix)

**Part 1: the matrix.**

"This is the map the exercise asked for. Each row is **who gets hurt**: the citizens, the ministry, the ad platform, and society. The columns run from **low to high severity**, and severity is simply **how likely** it is times **how bad** it would be, each scored 1 to 3. Those scores are **my judgement**, not measurements.

The pattern is clear: every **high**-severity risk lands on **the citizens or the ministry**. The platform never goes above medium, because it gets paid either way."

*(If someone asks about one risk, click its chip: a sheet opens with the scores, the evidence and the fix. Esc closes it.)*

**Part 2 (click): the fix.**

"So how do we fix it? The lecture's rule: it depends on whether anyone **meant it**.

**Deliberate** risks, like telling the buyer to chase the cheapest views, need a **policy and a named owner**: someone who signs off.

**Accidental** risks, like a wrong figure, need **checking and monitoring**.

And a French touch: don't stay online only. Put the same message in **CAF offices** and the **2,865 France Services** counters, the local public-service desks you see in these photos."

**Part 3 (click): the blue close.**

"So my recommendation comes down to **two things**.

One: put **a person between the two machines**, so the ad goes out checked, labelled as AI-made, and linked to the official Banque de France site. That's why the poster now has a green stamp: *vérifié par un humain*.

Two: judge the buyer by **who it reaches**, not by its average. A weekly report by income group, with a **minimum for each group**.

And that raises the next question: **what if agents ran this whole campaign?**"

## Slide 4, L'atelier agentique (the agentic workflow; press 4)

**Part 1: today's workflow, six cards.**

"Now let's look ahead. Next week's **Session 10 exercise** is to redesign a campaign workflow with agents, so let me do it for this same campaign. Today it moves **one team at a time**: the marketing manager writes the brief, creative drafts, legal reviews, media buys, it launches, and analytics judges it. Two problems. When legal finds an issue, the ad goes **back to creative** and waits again. And analytics judges the campaign **after it ends**, when the money's already spent. The zero percent from slide 2 would only show up there."

**Part 2 (click): the loop.**

"The lecture says: don't just **agentize**, speeding up each step. **Reinvent** the workflow. So the manager sets **the goal and the floors**: every income group, at least 60 percent. Then four agents run **one loop**. Create drafts the ad. Check verifies the figures and the AI label. Buy buys reach with a floor for every group. Measure reports by group every day and **feeds back** into Create. One step stays human: **the gate**, before any money is spent. And the new opportunities: legal checks happen inside the loop, results arrive while you can still act, there are variants for each region, and the same agents prepare kits for CAF counters."

**Part 3 (click): the human role.**

"So what's left for people? Not doing each step, but **guarding the loop**. An **approval gate** before spend. **Exceptions**: when a group falls below its floor, a person decides. A weekly **audit**, with the power to override. And what only people do: strategy, taste, and relationships with partners like the CAF and the Banque de France. The new risk is **linked agents**: one wrong figure spreads into every ad. That's why the gate sits right after the check. And my recommendation: build these agents on a **French, open-weight stack**, so the ministry can inspect the model and keep data in France. Here's one French option."

## Questions

**Ask Claude live (press Q).** The panel answers in two to four sentences, using only the deck's risks, scores, mock figures and 18 sources, with source numbers in brackets. Read the answer aloud or let the room read it. The first question asks you to **Allow**: do that before class (see the guide below).

**If you'd rather answer yourself:**

- **Where do the scores come from?** My judgement, using the lecture's formula: likelihood × impact, each 1 to 3. They're not measured, and the slide says so.
- **Is the reach problem proven for income?** No. It's proven for gender (the science-careers ad) and for gender and race (Facebook job and housing ads). For income it's a risk to test, which is why the first fix is a weekly report by group.
- **Are the campaign numbers real?** No. They come from the toy model on slide 2, and the slide says "mock".
- **How much would it take to reach everyone?** Annexe A: nobody rarely online is reached until **€264,000**, and everyone only at **€456,000**.
- **Why France, when the brief says "the government"?** I live there, and France has a real national financial-education strategy run by the Banque de France, so the scenario fits.
- **Backup slides:** A is the mock campaign report with the chart; B is the 18 sources and photo credits.

## Ask Claude: how it works (for you and the team)

1. **Open the claude.ai link**, signed in to your Claude account. The button only appears there, not in the downloaded HTML file.
2. **Before class, ask one test question.** The first question shows a permission prompt; click **Allow**. Each question uses the asker's own Claude usage.
3. **In class:** move the mouse, click **Ask Claude** (or press **Q**), tap a suggested question or type one, press Enter. An answer takes about 5 to 30 seconds.
4. **Ask by voice (Mac Dictation):** turn it on once in **System Settings → Keyboard → Dictation**, and add **French (France)** under its languages. In the deck, click in the question box, press the **🌐 Fn key twice**, speak, then press **Enter**. You can ask in French: Claude starts with "You asked:" and an English translation, then answers in English.
5. **Voice:** click **Voice: off** in the panel's top bar to switch it on, and Claude's answers are read aloud by the computer's own voice. Turn the room's speakers up before class. Combine it with Dictation (step 4) and the whole exchange is spoken.
6. **What it knows:** the deck (risks, scores, mock model, notes, sources, the Mistral facts), both exercises, Prof Osinga's slides for all ten sessions, and which slide is on screen. It cites them, e.g. "(Session 10, slide 37)". It doesn't browse the web and forgets everything when the page reloads.
7. **Check it:** Claude can be wrong. Its source numbers point to Annexe B.
8. **Teammates:** the artifact is private. Share it from the page's **Share** menu; each teammate must be signed in, and their questions use their own account.
